What makes commercial water damage different

A house has a few rooms and one owner. A building has tenants, shared walls, ceilings that connect floors and downtime that costs money. A leak in a second-floor suite can drip into three offices below. Restoration has to solve the water and keep the business moving.

Scale changes the plan

Large spaces need more equipment and a plan for airflow. Carpet tile is often lifted, dried and reused. Ceiling tiles that touch water are replaced. Phased drying lets part of a floor reopen while another part is still wet. Planning around tenants, delivery times and customer hours is part of the job.

Step 01.

Scope the loss.

Meters and cameras map wet areas across rooms and floors.

Step 02.

Extract and protect.

Water is removed and furnishings are lifted or covered.

Step 03.

Dry in phases.

Equipment is placed to reopen areas as they pass readings. See structural drying.

Step 04.

Document for claims.

Photos, moisture logs and equipment records support the insurer and the landlord.

Many commercial losses start in the plumbing. For a pipe failure, see burst pipe damage, and for storm-driven roof or window entry, see storm damage.

Catonsville commercial context

Catonsville is home to the CCBC Catonsville campus and sits next to the University of Maryland, Baltimore County, which enrolls roughly 14,000 students. Campuses, apartment buildings and shops with many rooms and shared systems are part of the local mix. Building permits are required for additions, alterations and structural modifications in Baltimore County, so ask whether a rebuild triggers one.

Records, inventory and equipment

Paper records, electronics and stock are often worth more than the carpet. Move them first if it is safe. The National Archives advises air drying wet paper within two days or freezing it, and cautions against aiming airflow directly at drying items. Do not touch the print surface of photographs or prints, and do not wash damaged images.

Computers and servers that got wet should stay off until a technician looks at them. Water in a power supply can turn a recoverable machine into a loss when someone switches it on to check.

Food and medication that touched water are discarded. A restaurant or clinic should speak to its regulator about what must be thrown out and what reopening requires.

Communicating with tenants and staff

A restoration plan works better when everyone knows the schedule. Post notices where equipment is running, keep walkways clear and name one contact person. Noise and airflow from drying equipment are normal, and tenants should know it is temporary.

For a multi-unit loss, record which units were affected and when. That helps the property manager, the tenants and the insurers sort responsibility later.

Commercial drying plans are built on the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, which covers water categories, equipment placement and documentation. Ask any contractor how the plan maps to it for your building.
